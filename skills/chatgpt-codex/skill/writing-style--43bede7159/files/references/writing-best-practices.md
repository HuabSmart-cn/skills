# Writing style best practices

Use this guide to improve the draft in the requested voice, not to replace that
voice with a house style. The user's instructions, required wording, audience,
and chosen examples control the result. These are contextual warning signs, not
a blacklist of words or punctuation. Revise patterns that are conspicuous,
repetitive, unearned, or make the meaning harder to understand.

Read the whole draft before editing. Focus on the prose the model controls;
preserve required wording, quotations, fixed labels, names, dates, formulas,
and other lookup data. Judge a short email on its own scale. Do not expand a
form, table, or concise notice just to make it look more like polished prose.
These checks assess the writing's usefulness, not whether a human or AI wrote it.

## Say something specific

Give the reader the actual point, decision, finding, or request. Name who did
what, what changed, why it matters, and what happens next when those details are
relevant and supported. Replace claims about "alignment," "impact," or a
"seamless experience" with the concrete result or reason. Explain a real
tradeoff rather than manufacturing balance. Do not substitute a description of
the review process for the reason behind a decision.

Keep claims proportional to their evidence. Distinguish facts, interpretations,
recommendations, and genuine uncertainty. Do not invent numbers, sources,
consensus, causes, owners, commitments, or certainty to make a sentence stronger.
Shortening a sentence must not remove a material condition, comparison, or
limitation.

Consider support across the whole document. A summary can rely on evidence,
analysis, an exhibit, or a qualitative example elsewhere. Do not demand a
number beside every claim or repeat the same evidence in every section. Keep
the basis findable and the conclusion no stronger than that basis allows.

## Remove manufactured rhetoric

Watch for habitual "not X, but Y" contrasts; "not only ... but also" formulas;
"the better framing is" pivots; "no X, no Y, just Z" constructions;
dramatic fragments; repeated three-part slogans; long rhythmic catalogues;
strained metaphors; canned empathy; inflated praise; vague appeals to experts;
and generic conclusions about challenges, significance, or the future. State
the underlying claim directly when the flourish adds nothing.

Use colons, semicolons, em dashes, parallelism, and bold-label bullets when they
clarify an actual relationship or help the reader scan. Do not use them
repeatedly to manufacture emphasis. A real contrast such as "The error is in
the import, not the calculation" is fine. So are deliberate warmth, humor,
informality, and distinctive habits supported by the author's examples.

## Use plain, accurate language

Prefer concrete subjects and direct verbs. Replace bureaucratic phrasing,
unexplained jargon, stacked abstractions, and excess hedging with the shortest
wording that keeps the meaning. Clusters of words such as "delve," "pivotal,"
"robust," "foster," and "landscape" deserve a second look; none is inherently
wrong. Keep useful technical, legal, clinical, or financial terms when they name
the right concept for the audience. Explain them when the reader needs help.
Preserve defined terms, legal qualifications, citations, and professional
shorthand when they improve precision. A shorter everyday synonym is not
automatically better. Replace ambiguous references such as "this approach" when
the reader would have to guess which decision or action they mean.

Illustrative edits; use any factual version only when the task supports it:

- "At this point in time, begin the review process" → "Review the draft now."
- "Proceed when five readiness gates are green" → "Proceed when all five
  launch criteria are met."
- "We aligned on the next phase" → "The team chose a limited pilot because the
  full rollout would exceed the budget."
- "Approve a stage-gated pilot; do not pre-authorize broad rollout" → "Approve
  a limited pilot and decide whether to expand after reviewing the results."

## Give the writing the right shape

When the genre permits, lead with the answer or decision. Use the lightest
structure that serves the reader: a short message may need a few sentences; a
specification, policy, or report may need substantial detail. Make headings
informative. Use lists for genuinely parallel items, steps, or comparisons.
Remove generic scene-setting, announcements about the writing process, repeated
recaps, empty transitions, and conclusions that add no information.
Keep purposeful repetition: an executive summary and a detailed analysis may
need to state the same recommendation for different readers. Keep framing that
narrows scope, explains an omission, or makes reference material easier to use.

Before delivery, reread for missing substance, awkward sentences, unsupported
claims, and conspicuous repeated patterns. Can the intended reader identify and
accurately restate the main points after one careful read? Check that the
revision still sounds like the selected author, preserves useful nuance, and
fulfills the actual request. Make the smallest edit that fixes a real reader
problem. When a supposed tic is debatable in context, leave it alone. Do not
flatten every sentence into the same short cadence. Keep this editorial pass
internal unless the user asks to see it.
